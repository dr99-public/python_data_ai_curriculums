
## Lesson Plan 1: Data and Analytics Foundations
- Understand the nature of data: formats, data types, driving factors behind transformations
- Learn basic tooling: spreadsheets, relational tables, basic SQL, basic BI
- Python data - how to execute a program, panda dataframes

These lessons will use a combination of tasks and examples developed for purpoase, coupled with publicly available videos, tutorials, and data sets. Limits of this curriculum:
- It can't possibly make someone an expert in data or analytics in a week, a month, or a year, as many courses promise to do.
- Not intended to teach database maintenance/administration, or formal Python programming.

Other data analysis (not coding/tech) skills:
- regression
Basic quantitative analysis skills
1. Descriptive statistics
These are the basics for summarizing data.
Key concepts:
count
sum
mean
median
mode
minimum / maximum
range
percentiles
quartiles
interquartile range
variance
standard deviation
The practical skill is knowing which summary is appropriate.
Example: income, order value, claim cost, and account balances are often skewed, so the median and percentiles may be more informative than the average

2. Distributions
Analysts need to understand the shape of data, not just single summary values.
Key concepts:
normal distribution
skewed distribution
long-tail distribution
uniform distribution
bimodal distribution
outliers
tails
concentration
Practical questions:
Are most values clustered tightly or spread out?
Is the average distorted by a few extreme values?
Are there two different populations mixed together?
Are the highest-value customers, claims, transactions, or cases driving most of the result?

3. Variability and dispersion
This is about how much values differ from each other.
Key concepts:
variance
standard deviation
coefficient of variation
interquartile range
z-scores
volatility
error bars
Practical use:
Two teams may have the same average productivity, but one may be highly consistent while the other swings wildly. The average alone hides that.

4. Probability basics
Analysts need enough probability to reason about uncertainty.
Key concepts:
probability
conditional probability
independence
expected value
base rates
odds
risk
false positives and false negatives
Practical use:
If a fraud model flags a transaction, the relevant question is not just “how accurate is the model?” It is also:
Given the low base rate of fraud, how likely is this flagged transaction actually fraudulent?

5. Sampling and sample size
A lot of bad analysis comes from overreading small samples.
Key concepts:
sample
population
sample size
sampling bias
random sampling
representativeness
margin of error
law of large numbers
Practical use:
A 30% increase among 12 customers may not mean much. A 3% increase among 2 million customers may be highly meaningful.

6. Confidence intervals
A point estimate is rarely enough.
Key concepts:
point estimate
confidence interval
margin of error
uncertainty range
confidence level
Practical use:
Instead of saying:
Conversion increased from 4.1% to 4.3%.
A better analyst asks:
Is that increase meaningfully outside the expected range of random variation?

7. Hypothesis testing
This is the formal version of “is this difference real or just noise?”
Key concepts:
null hypothesis
alternative hypothesis
p-value
statistical significance
practical significance
Type I error
Type II error
statistical power
Practical use:
A result can be statistically significant but operationally trivial. Or it can be practically important but not yet statistically proven because the sample is too small.

8. Correlation
Correlation is basic but often misused.
Key concepts:
positive correlation
negative correlation
correlation coefficient
scatterplots
spurious correlation
correlation vs causation
Practical use:
If customer tenure and spending are correlated, that does not prove tenure causes spending. It may be that better customers both stay longer and spend more.

9. Regression
Regression is one of the most useful general-purpose analytic concepts.
Key concepts:
simple linear regression
multiple regression
dependent variable
independent variable
coefficient
intercept
residual
R-squared
adjusted R-squared
multicollinearity
omitted variable bias
logistic regression
Practical use:
Regression helps estimate relationships while controlling for other factors.
Example:
Are higher discounts associated with higher sales after controlling for region, product, season, and customer size?
Regression does not automatically prove causality, but it is a core tool for structured analysis.

10. Residuals and model fit
Knowing how to evaluate a model matters as much as running one.
Key concepts:
residuals
fitted values
prediction error
overfitting
underfitting
model assumptions
goodness of fit
R-squared
mean absolute error
root mean squared error
Practical use:
A model can produce a clean-looking equation but still fail badly for certain segments, outliers, or ranges of values.

11. Time series analysis
Many business questions involve trends over time.
Key concepts:
trend
seasonality
cyclicality
moving average
rolling average
year-over-year comparison
month-over-month comparison
lag
leading indicator
trailing indicator
autocorrelation
forecast error
Practical use:
Comparing December to November may be useless in a seasonal business. Comparing December this year to December last year may be better.

12. Indexes and normalization
Analysts often need to put unlike things onto a comparable basis.
Key concepts:
index values
baseline period
normalization
standardization
per-capita measures
rate per unit
percent change
basis points
Practical use:
Instead of comparing raw claim counts across states, compare claims per 1,000 policies. Instead of comparing raw sales across stores, compare sales per square foot or sales per customer.

13. Ratios, rates, and percentages
This sounds simple, but it causes many mistakes.
Key concepts:
rate
ratio
percentage
percentage-point change
percent change
basis points
denominator effects
Practical use:
An increase from 2% to 4% is a 2 percentage-point increase and a 100% relative increase. Those are both true, but they communicate very different things.

14. Cohort analysis
Cohort thinking is essential for retention, customer behavior, performance tracking, and lifecycle analysis.
Key concepts:
cohort
retention curve
vintage
aging
survival
lifecycle
tenure
churn rate
Practical use:
Overall customer retention may look stable while newer customer cohorts are deteriorating. Aggregates can hide lifecycle effects.

15. Segmentation and stratification
This is partly conceptual and partly statistical.
Key concepts:
segment
stratum
subgroup
mix shift
Simpson’s paradox
weighted average
compositional effects
Practical use:
A company’s overall margin may decline even if every product’s margin improved, simply because the sales mix shifted toward lower-margin products.

16. Causality basics
Even before advanced causal inference, analysts need the fundamentals.
Key concepts:
causation
confounding
reverse causality
selection bias
natural experiment
control group
treatment group
before-and-after comparison
difference-in-differences
randomized controlled trial
A/B test
Practical use:
If customers who receive outreach are more likely to renew, that may be because outreach worked. Or it may be because sales teams selectively contacted customers who were already likely to renew.

17. Experimental design
This is the formal version of testing changes.
Key concepts:
randomization
control group
treatment group
sample size
statistical power
treatment effect
contamination
test duration
success metric
Practical use:
A bad A/B test can produce worse evidence than no test at all if the groups are not comparable or the measurement window is wrong.

18. Forecasting concepts
Not every analyst needs to build sophisticated forecasts, but most should understand the basics.
Key concepts:
forecast
baseline forecast
trend forecast
seasonality
forecast error
mean absolute percentage error
scenario analysis
sensitivity analysis
confidence bands
Practical use:
A forecast should usually be compared against a simple baseline. A complex model that cannot beat “same as last month” or “same month last year plus trend” is not adding value.

19. Sensitivity analysis
This is about understanding how conclusions change when assumptions change.
Key concepts:
assumption
parameter
scenario
best case
base case
worst case
elasticity
break-even point
Practical use:
If a business case only works when churn improves by 30%, acquisition cost falls by 20%, and prices rise by 10%, the conclusion is fragile.

20. Measurement error and bias
Analysts need to understand that data is not reality. It is a measurement of reality.
Key concepts:
measurement error
bias
missing data
survivorship bias
selection bias
recall bias
reporting bias
classification error
proxy variable
Practical use:
If customer satisfaction surveys only capture people angry enough or happy enough to respond, the results may not represent the full customer base.


A compact curriculum-style list
For basic analysis training, I would probably organize the quantitative concepts into this sequence:
Measures and distributions
Mean, median, percentiles, standard deviation, skew, outliers.
Rates and comparisons
Percent change, percentage points, ratios, indexes, denominators, baselines.
Variation and uncertainty
Sampling, confidence intervals, margin of error, statistical significance.
Relationships between variables
Correlation, scatterplots, simple regression, multiple regression, residuals.
Time-based analysis
Trends, seasonality, rolling averages, year-over-year comparisons, forecasting basics.
Segmentation and mix effects
Cohorts, stratification, weighted averages, Simpson’s paradox, composition effects.
Causal reasoning
Confounding, selection bias, control groups, A/B tests, difference-in-differences.
Decision analysis
Expected value, sensitivity analysis, scenario analysis, practical significance.
That is probably the right “actual analysis” layer between pure business reasoning and technical implementation.
